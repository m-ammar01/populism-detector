Populism Detector

Paste a paragraph of English political text. A small language model, running inside your browser, reads each sentence and compares it with example sentences written by undergraduate students for each of the five dimensions of populism. Your text is never uploaded. The first visit downloads the model (about 25 MB); your browser keeps it afterwards.

How this works

No chatbot is involved and nothing is sent anywhere. A small language model runs inside your browser, and the rest is arithmetic:

  1. Split your text into sentences. Fragments with fewer than three words are skipped.
  2. Encode. A compact neural network (all-MiniLM-L6-v2: six layers, 22 million parameters, trained by others on over a billion pairs of related sentences) reads each whole sentence and outputs 384 numbers that describe its meaning. Because it reads the sentence as a whole, word order and context influence the result, although negation, irony and quotation remain hard for it.
  3. Compare the sentence with two reference sets: the student-written examples for each dimension, and a set of neutral political and news sentences. The score for a dimension is how close the sentence is to that dimension’s examples minus how close it is to the neutral sentences.
  4. Calibrate. The raw score is converted into a percentile against what neutral sentences typically score, adjusting for the number of sentences (a long text has more chances to look populist by accident). That percentile is the “signal strength” on the chart.

The overall label then depends on the rule you choose. Under the default strict rule, a text is called populist-leaning only when all four core dimensions clear the threshold (vertical polarization, antagonism, Manichean valorization, volonté générale). Horizontal polarization is treated as the “thick” extension and is shown separately.

Limits of this prototype
  • It measures resemblance, not meaning. A sentence can resemble a populist example without being populist (quotation, irony, reporting about populism) and vice versa.
  • The neutral reference set is provisional. It was drafted by the developer, not collected from students, and the number of examples per dimension is small ( exemplars in total, a few flagged as borderline).
  • Telling dimensions apart is still only moderate. In internal tests the correct specific dimension was the model’s top choice about 39% of the time (unchanged from the first prototype) and among its top two about 73% (up from 61%). Chance is 20% and 40%.
  • The strict rule is demanding on short texts. The model separates the dimensions fairly cleanly, so a short paragraph often triggers only some of them. Many short populist passages therefore fail the strict test; the page tells you when the moderate rule would have counted the text. In a small developer-written test the moderate rule caught more populist paragraphs without flagging the neutral ones.
  • English only. The page warns you when a text does not look like English.
  • Do not use it to label real people or to grade work. It has not been validated against expert coding.
